My first blog! A little bit about myself, currently I'm a Data Scientist in Austin, TX. In my job I gain insights and develop scalable data
driven products. Before that I was a Quantitative Developer in the Energy industry. A Quantitative Developer or Quant is someone who codes and
develops pricing models and algorithms. I'm been very blessed and fortunate to have worked in both of these very interesting areas (Quant, Data Science) and its given me a unique perspective. So when I was thinking on what to write in a blog maybe I could combine methods from both of these disciplines and try to give new insights. Let me describe this further. 

In the past few years we have seen a new force that is "eating up the world", bringing both immense insight and productivity: **machine intelligence**. 
Shivon Zilis, was one of the first reporters to come up with this term "machine intelligence". Machine intelligence is a unifying term for what others call machine learning and 
artificial intelligence. Both of her articles [machine intelligence](https://medium.com/@shivon/the-current-state-of-machine-intelligence-f76c20db2fe1#.maunnvt97) 
and [machine intelligence 2.0](https://www.oreilly.com/ideas/the-current-state-of-machine-intelligence-2-0) are great 
on the current space and the ecosystem of companies that are growing in quantum leaps and bounds. One can see that in this new industry, the future and promise of automation is 
resting square "on the shoulders" of computational models.

Another industry that was highly model dependent was the derivative industry. The derivative industry has seen quantitative methods both good and bad. We all saw in 2007 
how a single way of viewing risk and profit could end in disaster. A good article on the modeling implications for this can be found in [Recipe for Disaster: The Formula That Killed Wall Street](https://www.wired.com/2009/02/wp-quant/). From this episode we also saw big differences in the way winning and losing firms viewed the management of 
their models and risk. Philippe Jorion writes a good article on these strategies: [Risk Management Lessons from the Credit Crisis](http://merage.uci.edu/~jorion/papers/RiskMgtCreditCrisis.pdf). In the report it showed a big difference in the culture of these firms, losing firms had risk management practices that had:

* organizations that were hierarchical

* no discussions

* relied on third parties for evaluations

* no testing of models and parameters

Winning firms had risk management practices that had:

* organizations that were cooperative

* shared information across the firm

* developed in-house expertise

* significant testing of their models and parameters

Winning firms had a test driven approach to their model development and execution. Models were not blindly trusted and the firm acted as a learning 
environment to improve and understand the limitations of these models. To me this seems very applicable to this current revolution in machine intelligence. 
Models will play a very important part in the not too distant future where we will have a highly connected 
world of drones, [IOT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_of_things) and machine based 
recommendations, in all phases of our lives. This world will be highly dependent on **production machine intelligence models**, to deliver these services.
Maybe we can gain some insights from the past as we develop these models in this new revolution. In **Test Driven Intelligence** I'm going to blog
about machine intelligence model development from the perspective of a winning risk management practice.
